One Percent

What would you charge one person, once?

Answer that, and the arithmetic does something uncomfortable: fifty thousand people at your number is a figure you've never let yourself write down. This is a call of up to half an hour about the number you just produced, and the first customer standing behind it.

$

$7 a session. Paid before the call. This call is worth a hundred dollars. It's seven because I'm one of the people I built it for. Seven is still real money when you have none, and it buys the half hour and your next steps in writing — not the work those steps describe. What that work costs is further down this page, before you pay anything.

What happens

  1. Up to thirty minutes, in your browser. Nothing to install, nothing to dial, no number and no app. You press a button on your own order page and it starts. It ends when the questions are answered, which is often sooner than the half hour.
  2. Eight questions, in order. What you need, what you can do, what you charge, the arithmetic run out loud on your own number, who the first paying customer is, what is standing in the way, and what can be arranged about that one thing.
  3. One action, with a name against it. Not five good ideas. One thing, and who is doing it. A session that ends in a list ends in nothing.

Those eight questions are written down and published in full — read the method before you pay. Nothing is held back for the session and nobody is screened for fitness. The only question is whether seven dollars is worth answering them and hearing what I make of your answers.

It is an automated assistant, not a person

It says so in its first sentence, and it says the session is recorded. A person reads everything it takes down.

It will not take an account of what has been done to you. If you start, it says one line and takes you back to the question rather than hanging up — you have paid for the half hour. That is deliberate. This is not therapy and a machine is not the listener.

It gives no advice, promises nothing, and decides nothing about you. It asks, it writes down what you said in your own words, and a person reads it.

What seven buys, and what it doesn't

I build software. The thing I've built is Skills Economy: a free site and phone app where Targeted Individuals list what they can do, find each other, and trade work between themselves. Twenty-eight separate tools sit inside it — a directory, a skills map, a board of work people need doing, a library, a radio station. Anyone can use it, it costs nothing, and the code is public for anyone to check.

Having that built for you would take a firm about two years and a team of five: two engineers, a product manager, a designer, and somebody keeping it running. A million and a half dollars, give or take. That's what it would cost to have built. It isn't what it would sell for — it's free and it's not for sale.

I built it on my own, in about a year, and sized it for five million people. What's sold here is that same work pointed at one person instead of five million — at you, and at whatever share of those five million you could serve yourself.

A plumber doesn't need twenty-eight tools. They need a way to be found and a way to keep track of the people who find them, and that gets built for them, at their own address, and it's theirs.

Seven dollars doesn't buy that. It buys the half hour and the next steps that come out of it, written down, whether the answer is go or no. You're not buying a service for seven dollars. You're finding out, for seven dollars, whether you want the rest.

Saying no to more costs you nothing you already had, and Skills Economy stays free and stays there either way.
